Introduction

Replicating PECD: physically-motivated features for forecasting Germany’s power market¶

The goal¶

The wider goal behind this project is forecasting the DE-LU bidding zone’s headline variables: wind generation, PV generation, and load (demand). This project itself is one building block toward that goal, not the end in itself — see How this project fits the wider goal below.

Current status: how well does each replication work?¶

All three workstreams are checked the same way in the end: weight a modeled capacity factor up to a national figure using MaStR’s own time-varying installed capacity, then compare against SMARD’s actual net generation over the full 2019-2025 backtest (61,368 hours). Three points of comparison throughout: PECD’s own official product (reweighted by MaStR capacity, not a replication at all — the best available anchor for “how good can this possibly get”), the simplest from-scratch physics baseline this project started from, and this project’s own best replication.

The remaining open item is a systematic high-wind over-estimate, most likely from the wake-effect/farm-layout modeling this project deliberately doesn’t attempt (see the roadmap below).

Wind offshore reaches the best correlation of the three (0.916), but a larger bias than either other approach: the same per-plant chain as onshore, adapted for two offshore-specific findings (GWA2’s bias-correction reference barely reaches past ~20km offshore; the shared storm-shutdown model over-derates in wind speeds offshore sees regularly but onshore rarely does — see the section below). Solar has closed much of the gap but not all of it: swapping in PECD’s fully documented PV conversion chain (Skartveit-Olseth decomposition, Klucher transposition, Martin-Ruiz optical losses, a Beyer et al. efficiency polynomial, Ross cell temperature, real per-plant orientation from MaStR) took correlation against SMARD from 0.926 to 0.937 and MAE from 0.057 to 0.046 — a real, year-over-year-consistent improvement over the naive baseline. But PECD’s own product is still roughly 3x closer to reality (MAE 0.016 vs. 0.046) than this project’s best replication gets. Open candidates, none conclusively resolved yet:

  • PECD’s documented inverter-loss polynomial (Schmidt & Sauer, 1994): applying it exactly as written makes the match against PECD’s own product worse, not better, so it’s currently omitted — but that may just trade one bias for another when checked against real generation instead (27).

  • The overestimate grows sharply with output level, plateauing around +0.08-0.09 capacity-factor bias above ~30% output — consistent with either missing inverter clipping or unmodeled self-consumption (rooftop generation used behind the meter, disproportionately during high-output hours) (29).

  • All three solar approaches — including PECD’s own official product — get measurably worse year over year, 2019 through 2025. Since that includes PECD’s own product, it isn’t a flaw in either physics chain specifically; more likely a growing self-consumption share, unmodeled panel degradation, or a shifting technology mix (29).

  • PECD’s documented 15-minute temporal downscaling step was implemented and tested — it makes no measurable difference, so it’s ruled out as an explanation for the remaining gap (27).

Why physical modeling helps: residual load¶

If we know the weather and which power plants exist, we can, in principle, compute power output directly from physical equations — no statistical model needed. Suppose demand were fully price-inelastic (fixed regardless of price). Then total generation must equal demand at every instant. If renewables generate less than is demanded, the gap — the residual load — has to be met by something else: coal or gas plants domestically, or imports (nuclear power from a neighboring country, for instance).

Residual load is, by definition, just demand minus renewable generation. So if we can forecast renewable generation well, we get residual load for free — which is arguably the more economically interesting quantity, since it’s residual load that drives prices, dispatch, and import/export flows.

Why it’s hard in practice¶

Even where the physical equations themselves are well understood (wind is the clearest example), several things stand between “we know the weather” and “we know the exact power injected into the grid”:

  1. High-wind shutdown. Turbines cut out above some wind speed for safety. Modelable in principle from wind speed alone, but PECD’s own methodology treats it as a per-turbine hysteresis process (shutdown and restart at different thresholds), which needs state we don’t have.

  2. Wind farm layout and wake effects. Where exactly a turbine sits inside its farm, and which direction the wind is blowing from, changes what wind speed it actually experiences — a front-row turbine sees different wind than one further back, whose approaching wind has already been slowed and turbulized by the rows ahead of it.

  3. Outages. Wind farms are sometimes offline entirely for maintenance or repair, independent of weather.

  4. Curtailment. When the grid is congested, some turbines aren’t allowed to feed in at all, regardless of how much wind is available. This is expected to become more common, not less, as renewable penetration grows.

  5. No ground truth for any of the above, individually. There is no published time series that isolates “generation lost to curtailment” or “generation lost to an outage” — only the net result reaches public data.

  6. The behind-the-meter blind spot. Grid statistics (SMARD, in this project’s case) only observe power in front of the meter — what actually crosses into the public grid. Anything happening behind the meter is invisible: if someone generates electricity and consumes it themselves, it shows up in neither the published generation figure nor the published demand figure. What we actually have access to is net generation and net demand, both already reduced by whatever self-consumption happened behind meters we can’t see.

    This has a subtle consequence for residual load specifically: since self-consumption is missing from both sides of the residual-load calculation in equal measure (the self-consumed MWh is absent from published generation, but also absent from published demand, since it was never drawn from the grid), the two omissions cancel and residual load itself stays largely correct. What breaks is any direct comparison of our own gross physical generation estimate against SMARD’s net generation figure — that comparison is not apples-to-apples unless self-consumption is explicitly accounted for.

What would a full replication need?¶

To compute gross wind production for a given weather situation, we need to know, precisely: (1) what the wind is, everywhere, and (2) exactly where which wind turbines stand, and which model they are.

  • For historical wind, the ERA5 reanalysis is the standard choice.

  • For plant location and turbine type, the Marktstammdatenregister (MaStR) is Germany’s own public register.

  • Alternatively, PECD (the Pan-European Climate Database) already does a version of this, at a European scale, with more elaborate methodology and proprietary inputs we don’t have access to.

PECD’s own methodology¶

PECD v4.2’s wind capacity factor pipeline, in order:

  1. Take ERA5 wind speed at 10m and 100m.

  2. Apply a bias adjustment using Global Wind Atlas (GWA) data — ERA5’s reanalysis wind speed is systematically off from observations in a way that varies by location; GWA’s higher-resolution product corrects this.

  3. Extrapolate to each turbine’s actual hub height, using a precomputed shear exponent (alpha) — wind speed doesn’t scale linearly with height, alpha captures how steeply it changes.

  4. Look up each location’s installed turbine type and compute a capacity factor from that turbine’s power curve.

  5. Correct for the wind farm’s actual layout — which turbines are in the first row versus further back, since row position changes the wind a turbine actually sees (wake effects).

  6. Apply high-wind shutdown assumptions, per turbine.

Our replication strategy¶

We rebuild this methodology with the means available to us: ERA5 for weather (same source PECD itself uses), and MaStR for plant location and turbine type (PECD uses a different, proprietary database — WindPowerNet — that we don’t have access to).

The hard part is that there is no single ground-truth time series against which to check whether the full replication is correct end to end. So instead of one validation, we built a ladder of checks, each isolating one step, each checked against whatever partial ground truth PECD itself publishes:

  1. Bias-adjusted wind speed vs. PECD’s own published bias-adjusted wind speed grid — a genuine, direct ground truth, since PECD publishes this as a gridded product in its own right.

  2. Hub-height extrapolation — no direct ground truth exists (PECD never publishes wind speed at arbitrary hub heights), so this is instead checked for internal consistency: since alpha was itself derived from the 10m/100m ratio, extrapolating our validated 100m field back down to 10m via alpha should closely reproduce our validated 10m field.

  3. Zone-level capacity factor (PEON zones, the only spatial aggregation PECD publishes for wind) vs. PECD’s own zone capacity factors — comparable either as relative capacity factors, or, weighted by MaStR’s installed capacity per zone, as absolute MW.

  4. National aggregate — MaStR’s zone-level installed capacity lets us weight the 7 PEON zones up to one Germany-wide number, comparable against the same aggregation applied to PECD’s own zone figures (since PECD itself publishes no ready-made national number for wind).

  5. SMARD’s actual net wind generation — the one point of comparison against real-world data, not just PECD’s own numbers. A deviation here can come from two different sources that are easy to conflate: our physical replication being imperfect, or curtailment/self-consumption creating a genuine gap between gross physical generation and what actually reaches the grid. Neither PECD’s product nor ours attempts to model curtailment, so some gap here is expected even from a perfect physical replication.

How this project fits the wider goal¶

Three different ways of turning weather + MaStR capacity into a physically- motivated wind feature have been built (see Comparing all three approaches):

  1. PECD’s own capacity factor, reweighted by MaStR capacity — trusts PECD’s official numbers wholesale.

  2. A simple from-scratch physics model (one generic turbine curve, raw ERA5, no bias correction) — the simplest possible replication.

  3. This project’s full replication — GWA-bias-adjusted wind speed, alpha-based hub-height extrapolation, per-plant power curves matched to real turbine models where possible.

None of these three is “the answer” in isolation — the actual target is forecasting headline variables well, and any feature that helps with that is worth keeping even where it deviates from PECD’s own methodology. Replicating PECD as closely as possible is the validation exercise that tells us how good a candidate feature is, not the end goal itself.

Wind offshore replication¶

Same ladder as onshore (per-plant power curves from MaStR, PECD’s alpha grid for hub-height extrapolation, checked against PECD’s own zone product then against SMARD’s actual wind_offshore generation), but two of onshore’s steps needed a genuine methodology change rather than a copy-paste, both found by checking intermediate results against real numbers rather than assumed to transfer directly from onshore:

  • GWA2 barely covers the German offshore fleet. GWA’s own downscaling (WAsP) explicitly excludes “offshore areas beyond 20 km” (per the PUG). Checked against MaStR’s actual offshore turbine coordinates: 91% of installed German offshore capacity sits at grid cells with no GWA2 delta-correction factor at all. Cross-checked against PECD’s own downloaded bias-adjusted wind-speed product vs. raw ERA5 at those same deep-sea cells: the ratio is ~1.0000 beyond ~20km from shore (real correction only applies near-shore) — PECD’s own product evidently leaves deep-offshore wind speed uncorrected too. The replication matches that: apply the GWA delta where available, leave raw ERA5 speed unmodified elsewhere.

  • The shared storm-shutdown model over-derates offshore. Its linear ramp (20→25 m/s) is tuned against onshore, where hub-height wind speed rarely exceeds 20 m/s; offshore it regularly does (checked: ~3.8% of hours at a representative North Sea cell across 2022 fall in that 20-25 m/s band, vs. essentially never onshore). Applied as written it derated a January storm snapshot to a third of PECD’s own product’s output at that hour, even though the pre-shutdown power curve alone already tracked PECD’s product closely (0.91 vs. 0.86) — same pattern as solar’s inverter-loss polynomial: implemented elsewhere (pecdr/shutdown.py), demonstrably doing net harm here, so left out of the offshore chain with the discrepancy documented rather than patched with an unvalidated offshore-specific threshold.

Offshore turbine technical detail in MaStR is markedly more complete than onshore’s — 100% of active offshore capacity has a known turbine model (vs. ~44-56% onshore) and 97.6% a known hub height — though only ~2.5% of that capacity matches an exact public OEDB power curve (vs. ~44% onshore, most offshore models being larger/newer than the library covers), so the generic specific-power-parametrized fallback curve carries most of the offshore fleet.

Against SMARD’s actual generation, this project’s replication reaches the best correlation of the three (0.916, edging out even PECD’s own official product’s 0.902) but with a larger absolute bias (+0.108 vs. PECD’s +0.073 and the naive baseline’s +0.071) — it tracks the true up-and-down weather pattern slightly better than PECD’s own product does, but systematically overestimates the level more. Plausible drivers, none isolated yet: German offshore wind has well-documented real-world curtailment from delayed grid connections (farms commissioned before their export cable is ready), which neither this replication nor PECD’s own product attempts to model — the same “no ground truth for curtailment” gap flagged for onshore above, likely larger here since offshore grid-connection delays are a well-known, material issue in the German market.

Is the bias just our own capacity accounting being wrong, rather than curtailment? The “PECD official x MaStR” comparison above leans on this project’s own MaStR-derived monthly PEOF-zone capacity panel for weighting on both sides — so a wrong capacity vector could manufacture that same bias with no real curtailment involved. Checked directly: solving for the per-zone capacity vector that best reconstructs SMARD’s actual generation from PECD’s own zone capacity-factor shapes (non-negative, monotonically non-decreasing, via NNLS on a reparametrized design matrix — see notebook 35) drives the bias to roughly zero (-0.03 vs. MaStR weights’ +0.16 in MW terms, same 3-zone basis), confirming a real chunk of the bias is attributable to our own capacity numbers, not to PECD or curtailment. But correlation barely moves (0.909 vs. 0.898) — reweighting capacity fixes the level, not the shape. Since the fitted vector is by construction the best any monotonic capacity story can do, that remaining gap can’t be closed by better fleet data — it needs something the capacity vector structurally can’t represent: curtailment or another dynamic effect. The fitted national capacity trajectory is also strikingly different in shape from MaStR’s: essentially flat 2020-2024 while MaStR’s nameplate figure climbs steadily, only converging in 2025 — the same window Germany’s offshore grid connections (BorWin5/DolWin6-era converter platforms) were catching up with the capacity build-out, consistent with, though not proof of, a growing grid-bottleneck story.

One more data-quality finding along the way, worth flagging on its own: 3 of PECD’s 6 PEOF zones (DE013_OFF/DE014_OFF/DE015_OFF) are 100% NaN in PECD’s own product across the entire 2015-2025 file — PECD apparently never modeled them. DE013_OFF still carries real MaStR capacity (up to ~300 MW), which was silently diluting the “PECD official x MaStR” capacity-weighted denominator above with dead-weight capacity that contributes nothing to the numerator — a small (~1.5-3% of national capacity) but real effect, separate from the capacity-vector question above. Fixed in notebook 34 by restricting that comparison to the zones PECD actually has data for; the numbers already quoted throughout this page reflect the fix. Removing the dilution moved “PECD official x MaStR” bias up (+0.060 → +0.073), not down — the dilution had been partially masking, not inflating, the true bias.

Solar PV replication¶

Same goal as wind — a physically-motivated PV generation feature, checked against PECD’s own product and then against SMARD’s real generation — but starting from a harder position: MaStR mostly lacks per-plant coordinates for solar (~95% missing), so weather has to stay at NUTS2 region resolution rather than per-plant grid cells, and PV physics has more conversion steps than a wind turbine’s power curve (irradiance decomposition, plane-of-array transposition, optical losses, module efficiency, cell temperature, inverter losses).

PECD’s own methodology (v4.2 Product User Guide)¶

  1. Decompose horizontal irradiance (GHI) into beam and diffuse components (Skartveit-Olseth, 1987).

  2. Transpose onto the panel’s actual plane of array — Klucher’s (1979) anisotropic sky model for diffuse sky radiation, plus a fixed-albedo ground-reflected term.

  3. Apply optical (reflection) losses at the panel surface (Martin-Ruiz).

  4. Convert plane-of-array irradiance to module efficiency at 25°C via a 5-coefficient polynomial (Beyer et al., 2004), fit by PECD against real German/French generation data.

  5. Correct for cell temperature (Ross, 1976) and a linear thermal derating coefficient.

  6. Apply inverter losses via a 3-coefficient polynomial (Schmidt & Sauer, 1994).

  7. A two-step temporal downscaling (hourly GHI/TA interpolated to 15-minute resolution, capacity factor computed at that finer step, then averaged back to hourly) to better capture sunrise/sunset variability.

Our replication strategy¶

Two versions have been built, in sequence:

  1. A simple baseline (pecdr/physics.py) — Erbs decomposition, one fixed tilt/azimuth for all of Germany, Faiman cell temperature, a generic PVWatts DC model. No technology split.

  2. The full PECD-documented chain (pecdr/solar_config_physics.py) — every step above except inverter losses (see below), using each MaStR unit’s actual reported orientation and tilt bucket (pecdr/solar_orientation.py) split across PECD’s 4 technology classes (industrial/residential rooftop, utility fixed/tracking).

The documented inverter-loss polynomial (step 6) is implemented (INVERTER_COEFS in pecdr/solar_config_physics.py) but not applied: taken exactly as written and checked against the raw LaTeX source of the PUG (not a lossy prose extraction), it behaves like roughly capacity-factor squared across the operating range — a ~50% loss at half load, far more than any real inverter — and makes the match against PECD’s own product markedly worse rather than better. Left out, with the discrepancy flagged in the code rather than silently resolved. The temporal-downscaling step (step 7) is implemented (pecdr/temporal_downscaling.py) and tested, and makes no measurable difference once an initial phase-mismatch bug (an hourly-average GHI value divided by an instantaneous clear-sky irradiance sample near sunset) was found and fixed.

Prerequisite step, no notebook of its own: pipeline/24 crosswalks MaStR units to PECD’s 4 PV technology codes (industrial/residential rooftop, utility fixed/tracking — MaStR has no such labels natively) and builds a monthly (NUTS2 zone × technology) installed-capacity panel used throughout everything below.

Congestion & curtailment: what the data shows¶

Before trusting any estimated curtailment model, it’s worth looking at real ground truth — from two sources with very different tradeoffs, described and then reconciled across four notebooks.

The first notebook covers SMARD’s monthly series: TSO+DSO combined, folds in countertrading and grid-reserve volume (not redispatch-only), and reflects a settled, ex-post counterfactual (“Ausfallarbeit”) calculation rather than a live instructed-power reading — real, but coarse (monthly, national-only). Wind dominates curtailment volume, offshore wind is curtailed roughly 8-9x more heavily per MW installed than onshore, and PV’s smaller curtailment tracks its own generation season.

The second notebook covers a much richer, per-measure source netztransparenz.de also publishes (real timestamps, per-plant/cluster names) — TSO-issued, per-measure frequency, but only comprehensively technology-labeled for offshore wind; onshore wind and PV are almost never named individually.

The third notebook reconciles the two: the aggregate gap is concentrated in the countertrading/unknown category (only ~13% of SMARD’s figure is captured), not spread evenly; offshore wind matches almost exactly (100%) at the technology level; and most of the per-measure file’s unlabeled majority can be attributed statistically (a seasonal-fingerprint fit per grid-connection entity — geographically sensible, wind-dominated in the north and PV-dominated in Bavaria, without ever being told any geography) after a MaStR-based join attempt turned out to be a clean dead end. An important caveat carries through: this attribution’s absolute levels still run about a third below SMARD’s, so it’s a better source for shape than for absolute levels.

That shortfall can be seen directly, before any wind/PV attribution ever runs: summing onshore wind and PV together (everything in Erneuerbar that isn’t offshore) against SMARD’s own combined Wind_Onshore + Photovoltaik total needs no attribution guessing at all, and still only captures 38.7% of SMARD’s figure over the full period — confirming the under-coverage is a real volume problem, not an artifact of splitting wind from PV. The monthly ratio between the two is also far from a stable scale factor: over 100x in mid-2022, settling to a calmer ~1.5-8x only from late 2023 onward, tracking Redispatch 2.0’s renewable reporting as it matured rather than a fixed measurement bias.

The fourth notebook uses offshore wind’s now-trusted curtailment numbers to check a claim made earlier, in the residual-load walkthrough: that offshore wind’s raw potential-observed gap is “(almost) entirely loss.” Real curtailment explains only ~40% of that gap on average. For what’s left, negative day-ahead prices — a second, price-driven form of voluntary curtailment that never shows up in either redispatch source — turn out to carry real signal: splitting days by whether they had a negative-price hour drops the curtailment-only model’s fit from R² = 0.89 to R² = 0.60, and a simple regression of the curtailment-unexplained residual on negative-price exposure comes back with a positive slope (~0.43) but a modest R² (0.26). Negative prices are a real, measurable contributor — not the dominant one. What’s left after both mechanisms points at bias in this project’s own offshore “potential” replication as the more likely explanation for the remainder.

The fifth notebook asks the analogous question for onshore wind, whose reconstruction (unlike offshore’s) sits well below SMARD’s reported total — only 45.1%, once offshore’s perfect match is excluded from chapter three’s blended 67.5% renewables figure. Rather than accept that shortfall, it tests whether a pure monthly level correction (rescaling the reconstruction so its monthly total matches SMARD’s exactly) closes the potential-observed gap as well as offshore’s real curtailment did. It doesn’t: R² against y = x only rises from ~0.65 to ~0.75 (offshore went from 0.21 to 0.90), even though the monthly level now matches SMARD by construction. The recalibration factor itself is revealing — implausibly large (up to ~80x) in 2022, settling to a plausible ~1.2-6x only from late 2023 onward — meaning the early shortfall isn’t a stable scale problem so much as a sign that Redispatch 2.0’s renewable reporting was still ramping up. The conclusion: onshore’s problem isn’t just missing volume, it’s that the timing of what’s currently captured is itself an imperfect proxy — pointing back at the entity-level attribution problem (chapter 46) rather than a simple correction factor.

Residual load: demand and curtailment estimation¶

The wind and solar workstreams above check generation against SMARD. The wider project goal, stated at the top of this page, is a day-ahead residual-load forecast — which needs two things that are never directly observed: gross power demand (grid-metered load plus invisible behind-the-meter self-consumption) and renewable generation net of curtailment. A walkthrough page teaches this model start to finish — the problem, the mental model, the equations, and the evidence, in that order — and a dedicated reference page writes down the full system of equations relating these hidden variables to what this project can actually observe. This section summarizes what came out of estimating them.

The gross-demand regression (notebook 38) fits grid-metered load as a calendar/temperature demand model minus PV self-consumption, identified by a simple structural fact: behind-the-meter self-consumption can only exist when PV is producing, so nighttime and heavily overcast hours pin down the demand-model coefficients with zero interference, while daytime hours (where PV output varies a lot day to day from cloud cover, at fixed calendar/weather conditions) pin down the self-consumption effect — one regression, not two sequential models. Both of its built-in falsification checks (a feed-in category that structurally cannot self-consume; wind potential, which has no behind-the-meter channel at all) came back significant rather than the expected zero — investigated rather than brushed aside: the PV category split turned out to be a genuine non-identification problem (the two candidate regressors correlate at 0.95), while wind’s effect survived a direct test of the obvious alternative explanation (a richer temperature spline barely changed it) and is more likely a real, different phenomenon — price-responsive industrial demand rising during cheap, high-wind hours — that the test’s own premise didn’t anticipate. The aggregate specification’s self-consumption estimate (~12% of national PV potential, net of the ~40% of the fleet that’s registered full-feed-in and structurally can’t self-consume at all) is this notebook’s trustworthy headline number.

Curtailment estimation (notebook 39) calibrates a curtailment-rate-vs- oversupply curve on wind’s own gap — clean because wind has no behind-the-meter term to confound it — using a potential-based oversupply proxy (P_total/D_true_hat, not the observed-generation-based proxy from the wind-offshore workstream’s own bias-driver notebook, which understates true oversupply exactly when curtailment is largest) and transfers that curve to PV. The result reframes what’s driving PV’s gap against SMARD: of its ~2177 MW average gap, ~1653 MW (76%) is predicted curtailment — more than self-consumption, which wasn’t obvious going in. The residual left over correlates with the self-consumption-registered capacity share at 0.643, though both series simply trend upward together over 2019-2025, so that number is suggestive rather than a tight validated match.

The residual-load backtest (notebook 40), trained on 2019-2023 and evaluated out-of-time on 2024-2025 against realized residual load (computable directly from SMARD, no new data needed), shows the full chain roughly halves the MAE of the naive baseline that skips all of this hidden-variable estimation (4,856 MW vs. 10,560 MW) and cuts the bias by roughly 5x (-1,936 MW vs. -9,750 MW), with a real but smaller correlation improvement (0.915 vs. 0.905) — consistent with the demand and curtailment corrections fixing the level more than the day-to-day pattern, which both approaches already capture reasonably well from shared weather inputs. (Caught and fixed while building this comparison: the curtailment curve originally returned NaN for any out-of-time hour whose oversupply proxy fell outside the training period’s own range — 4.1% of the test period — silently dropped rather than handled; fixed in pecdr/residual_load.py by clipping into the fitted range, the same graceful-degradation behavior a tree-based model gives for free.)

Does the structure actually earn its keep, though? (notebook 42) splits “does weather information help” from “does explicitly modeling unobserved quantities help on top of that,” with four more points on the same out-of-time backtest: a calendar-only floor with no weather at all, the same features as the full model fed into one flat linear regression with no structural decomposition, the same features again fit with LightGBM, and a capacity-factor-rescaled version of the LightGBM model. The answers are not what a reader might expect: adding raw weather-derived potential without correcting it for curtailment (the naive baseline) is no better than no weather information at all in absolute-error terms (10,560 MW MAE vs. 10,284 MW for calendar-only) — correlation improves a lot (0.905 vs. 0.486) but the uncorrected bias overwhelms that benefit. More surprisingly, a plain linear regression given the same inputs as the full structural model, with none of the hand-built equations, does better than the structural model on this backtest (MAE 4,177 MW, corr 0.941, vs. the structural model’s 4,856 MW / 0.915) — in this setting, most of the benefit comes from having the right features at all, not from the specific structural machinery used to extract them. The LightGBM version underperforms the linear one, for a concrete, checked reason rather than a mystery: installed capacity grew throughout 2019-2025, so years_since_start has 100% of test-period rows beyond the training range and P_wind_off_mw has 20% — tree-based models cannot extrapolate a trend past what they were trained on, while a linear trend term does so by construction.

Does capacity-factor rescaling fix LightGBM’s handicap, as expected? Partially, not fully. A sixth model swaps P_i(t) for capacity factor (P_i(t)/Capacity_i(t), bounded regardless of fleet size) as LightGBM’s features and predicts ResidualLoad(t)/TotalCapacity(t) instead of MW directly, reconstructing the final MW prediction by multiplying back by the actual (looked-up, not modeled) capacity at the test time. This narrows the gap substantially (MAE 6,714 → 5,294 MW) — confirming the extrapolation story really was the mechanism — but doesn’t reach M3 or even M2. Checked directly rather than left unexplained: the model’s error in the normalized domain is tiny and essentially unbiased in-sample (~0.000), but picks up a small out-of-sample bias (~0.006) on the test period, and that small bias gets amplified when multiplied back by TotalCapacity_mw to reconstruct MW — confirmed by a 0.44 correlation between the signed MW error and total capacity at test time. Rescaling fixes the tree’s input-side extrapolation problem; it trades in a smaller, different amplification effect on the output-reconstruction side instead.
