Congestion data: how much curtailment actually happens

Every curtailment number in the residual-load chapter that follows this one is estimated — a curve fit to wind’s own potential-vs-observed gap, transferred to PV under an assumption that’s never been checked against real data. This chapter comes first for a reason: before trusting any model of curtailment, it’s worth looking at real ground truth and building a feel for how much of it there actually is, and when.

Where this data comes from¶

Since July 2022, SMARD — the Bundesnetzagentur’s electricity market data platform, already this project’s source for generation and load — publishes monthly volumes of feed-in that was adjusted under Germany’s Redispatch 2.0 congestion-management regime, broken down by energy carrier (wind onshore, wind offshore, solar, and the conventional sources). Two directions are published: reductions (feed-in cut below what a plant could have produced — curtailment) and increases (counter-trading up-regulation, plants asked to produce more to compensate). Both are pulled by pecdr/smard_redispatch.py and saved by pipeline/43_download_redispatch_by_source.py.

Unlike the rest of this project’s SMARD data, this series has no documented API — the frontend multiplexes roughly 130 unrelated chart series into one shared CSV, selected client-side by a chart_id column. The download module re-derives the CSV’s current URL from the live redispatch topic page each run (rather than hardcoding it), since that URL is a CMS resource ID that can change whenever the site is republished.

What this data can and can’t tell us¶

It can: give real, technology-labeled, monthly curtailment volumes covering July 2022 onward, at national scale. That’s enough to check relative magnitudes between technologies and broad seasonal patterns.

It can’t: resolve anything at hourly or regional detail — there’s no way to see which hour or which grid region drove a given month’s number, so it can’t directly recalibrate the hourly oversupply-proxy curve the residual-load model uses. It also only covers redispatch (grid-operator-ordered curtailment) — market-based curtailment, where a plant operator voluntarily cuts output because of negative prices, is a separate mechanism entirely and doesn’t appear in this data at all. And it starts in July 2022, so it can’t cover the earlier years of this project’s 2019-2025 backtest.

What the data shows¶

Wind — onshore and offshore together — dominates curtailment volume, and both show a clear seasonal rhythm: more curtailment in the windier autumn/winter months. PV’s curtailment is smaller in absolute terms but just as clearly seasonal, tracking its own generation season instead.

The standout finding: wind offshore’s cumulative curtailment (16,108 GWh) is actually larger than wind onshore’s (12,873 GWh), despite an installed offshore fleet only about a seventh the size of onshore’s (~9.2 GW vs. ~63.1 GW in 2025). Per MW installed, that’s roughly 8-9x more curtailment for offshore than onshore — consistent with known transmission bottlenecks moving generation from the northern coast to load centers further south and west. This project’s residual-load model currently pools onshore and offshore wind into a single curtailment curve (notebooks 39/40/42) — this asymmetry is a real pattern that pooling discards.

As a sanity check on the data’s own labeling: renewable sources sit at (or extremely close to) 100% “reduction” share of their total redispatch volume, while conventional/flexible sources (hard coal, gas, pumped storage) split much more evenly between reductions and increases — exactly what the reduction/increase terminology should imply if it means what it says.

What’s next¶

This chapter deliberately stops at description — no tie-back to this project’s own curtailment estimates. The natural next question, left open here on purpose, is whether this real data actually supports the wind-to-PV curve-transfer assumption the residual-load model relies on, or whether it argues for a PV-specific curve instead. See the residual-load walkthrough and the system-of-equations reference for where that assumption is used and why it was made in the first place.
