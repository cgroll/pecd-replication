Foundations: from grid demand to residual load

This chapter is self-contained: every symbol is defined where it first appears, and nothing here depends on having read anything else in this book. It builds up, step by step, one growing diagram of how Germany’s electricity system fits together — starting from the one number everyone already has an intuition for (how much power the grid delivers) and ending at the quantity this project actually wants to forecast: residual load, the part of demand that has to be met by dispatchable generation once weather-driven renewables have done what they can.

The notation matches pecdr/residual_load.py and the more exhaustive equations reference page one-for-one, so nothing has to be relearned if you already know those — but nothing here assumes you do. Where this chapter simplifies or omits something the reference page covers in full (behind-the-meter battery dynamics, the feed-in-category split, a dead-end derivation worth knowing about), it says so and points there.

Step 1: what the grid actually sees — a balance, not a model¶

Start with the one quantity that’s simply measured, not estimated: D_grid(t), SMARD’s published grid load at hour t — how much power was drawn from the German public grid. Every megawatt of it has to come from somewhere: wind onshore, wind offshore, solar PV, or everything else (hydro, fossil, biomass, nuclear until 2023, geothermal — grouped here as G_other(t), since this project doesn’t model a weather-driven “potential” for any of it), plus whatever crosses the border.

Dgrid(t)=Gwind_on(t)+Gwind_off(t)+Gpv(t)+Gother(t)+(Gstorage,discharge(t)−Gstorage,charge(t))+NetImport(t)D_{grid}(t) = G_{wind\_on}(t) + G_{wind\_off}(t) + G_{pv}(t) + G_{other}(t) + \big(G_{storage,discharge}(t) - G_{storage,charge}(t)\big) + NetImport(t)Dgrid​(t)=Gwind_on​(t)+Gwind_off​(t)+Gpv​(t)+Gother​(t)+(Gstorage,discharge​(t)−Gstorage,charge​(t))+NetImport(t)
(1)

This is an accounting identity, true by construction — nothing here is estimated or forecast, it’s just bookkeeping. It also settles a question that’s easy to get tangled in: does exporting power count as part of “demand”? No — NetImport(t) is signed. When Germany is a net exporter, NetImport(t) < 0, and the identity says domestic generation has to cover both D_grid(t) and the amount leaving the country. Export doesn’t split D_grid(t)'s stacked bar; it sits on the generation side of the same balance, as a negative import.

Grid-scale storage (pumped hydro, increasingly grid batteries) is real and already in this identity, but genuinely just mentioned here — not pulled into anything that follows.

Step 2: residual load — one specific way of splitting that sum¶

Of the terms in Step 1’s identity, three are weather-driven and variable: wind onshore, wind offshore, and PV. Their output depends on what the weather happens to be doing, not on a dispatch decision. Everything in G_other(t) — plus imports — is treated in this project as dispatchable: available more or less on demand, within its own constraints, and (for domestic fossil generation specifically) what actually sets the price in a merit-order market.

Residual load is defined as what’s left of grid demand once those three weather-driven technologies are subtracted:

ResidualLoad(t)=Dgrid(t)−(Gwind_on(t)+Gwind_off(t)+Gpv(t))ResidualLoad(t) = D_{grid}(t) - \big(G_{wind\_on}(t) + G_{wind\_off}(t) + G_{pv}(t)\big)ResidualLoad(t)=Dgrid​(t)−(Gwind_on​(t)+Gwind_off​(t)+Gpv​(t))
(2)

This is a scope decision worth naming, not hiding. Hydro is partly weather-driven too (run-of-river especially), and PECD does publish hydro capacity factors — this project just doesn’t currently model hydro potential or subtract it here. It’s grouped into G_other(t) alongside genuinely dispatchable fossil and biomass generation. That’s a reasonable simplification (German hydro is a small share of the mix, and much of it — reservoir and pumped storage — really is dispatchable), but it means “residual load” here is narrower than “load minus all renewables”; it’s specifically “load minus the two technologies this project models a weather-driven potential for.”

Step 3: what’s actually invisible — behind-the-meter, on both sides¶

D_grid(t) and every G_i(t) above are net of something neither side publishes: behind-the-meter (BTM) self-consumption — power generated and used locally, never touching the public grid. It’s asymmetric across technologies: Germany’s wind fleet is essentially 100% utility-scale and always grid-connected, so BTM_{wind\_on}(t) ≈ BTM_{wind\_off}(t) ≈ 0 structurally. PV is different — MaStR (Germany’s public plant register) splits PV capacity into a full_feed_in category (required to export 100%, no self-consumption possible by regulation) and self-consumption categories, and BTM is concentrated in the latter — measured from the registry, not assumed, and it skews toward smaller rooftop installations rather than utility-scale plants.

What’s physically produced splits into what reaches the grid and what’s used behind the meter:

Producedi(t)=Gi(t)+BTMi(t)Produced_i(t) = G_i(t) + BTM_i(t)Producedi​(t)=Gi​(t)+BTMi​(t)
(3)

And on the demand side, the mirror image: grid-metered demand plus the invisible BTM demand met by local self-consumption is gross demand, never directly observed:

Dtrue(t)=Dgrid(t)+Dbtm(t)D_{true}(t) = D_{grid}(t) + D_{btm}(t)Dtrue​(t)=Dgrid​(t)+Dbtm​(t)
(4)

(BTM_pv(t) can also be time-shifted through a paired rooftop battery — same-hour self-use plus battery discharge minus battery charge, with its own state-of-charge recursion. Real, and mentioned for completeness, but not modeled anywhere below; see the equations reference page for the full battery notation if needed.)

Putting the whole thing together — potential generation splitting into losses (next step) and what’s produced, produced splitting into grid and behind-the-meter, and D_grid(t) (from Step 1’s balance) sitting alongside gross demand:

This is the full structural picture the rest of this chapter fills in. Read it left to right: potential splits into loss streams and what’s actually produced; produced splits again into grid-injected and behind-the-meter; the behind-the-meter piece reappears on the demand side, combining with D_grid(t) into gross demand D_true(t). ResidualLoad(t) only ever touches the grid boxes — which is exactly why BTM turns out to cancel out of it (Step 9), even though it still has to be modeled correctly to get D_true(t) right along the way (Step 6).

Step 4: why bother modeling any of this at all¶

Everything in the diagram above except P_i(t), the loss terms, BTM, and D_true(t) is something SMARD already publishes. ResidualLoad(t) itself can be computed today, directly, from already-published numbers — no modeling needed, just Step 2’s subtraction.

The reason to go further is that the actual goal is a day-ahead forecast of residual load — tomorrow’s number, before SMARD has published anything for tomorrow. And the already-published quantities are exactly the ones you can’t forecast directly from weather and calendar, because they’re already contaminated by things that haven’t happened yet from a forecaster’s point of view: G_i(t) is already net of curtailment nobody has decided on yet, and D_grid(t) is already net of self-consumption that depends on tomorrow’s cloud cover. What can be forecast from weather and calendar are the underlying physical and behavioral quantities: P_i(t) (what the fleet would produce, given a weather forecast) and D_true(t) (what people and industry actually want to consume, given a calendar and a temperature forecast). The next few steps build both.

Step 5: potential generation — what the fleet would produce, absent any losses¶

P_i(t) is this project’s own modeled, weather-driven technical potential for technology i ∈ {wind_on, wind_off, pv} — what the installed fleet would generate given the weather, before any real-world loss. It’s built from ERA5 reanalysis weather (bias-corrected against the Global Wind Atlas for wind, extrapolated to each turbine’s hub height) and MaStR’s own record of which plants exist, where, and of what type — the same replication effort documented in this book’s wind and solar workstreams, cross-checked against PECD’s own published capacity factors at every step. P_total(t) = P_{wind\_on}(t) + P_{wind\_off}(t) + P_{pv}(t).

Potential splits into real-world losses plus whatever’s actually produced (plus this project’s own model error, kept separate on principle even though it’s empirically indistinguishable from real losses without independent validation):

Pi(t)=Lrepair,i(t)+Lprice,i(t)+Lcongestion,i(t)+Producedi(t)+εi(t)P_i(t) = L_{repair,i}(t) + L_{price,i}(t) + L_{congestion,i}(t) + Produced_i(t) + \varepsilon_i(t)Pi​(t)=Lrepair,i​(t)+Lprice,i​(t)+Lcongestion,i​(t)+Producedi​(t)+εi​(t)
(5)

  • L_repair,i(t) — technical unavailability: storm damage, cable faults, scheduled maintenance. Independent of price or grid signals.

  • L_price,i(t) — voluntary curtailment during negative prices. Not a grid-operator order, and not in any published redispatch dataset.

  • L_congestion,i(t) — grid-operator-enforced curtailment (Redispatch 2.0 / Einspeisemanagement). The one loss category with published official data — but only from October 2021, and even then only monthly and national-only (see notebook 44 for what that data does and doesn’t cover).

Looking at the gap between potential and observed generation directly is the first evidence this isn’t just bookkeeping:

Wind’s gap is (almost) pure loss, since BTM_{wind}(t) ≈ 0 — that’s what makes it the clean calibration signal used in Step 8. PV’s gap mixes loss and self-consumption together; separating them needs Step 6 and Step 8 both.

Step 6: gross demand — what demand would be if every meter were visible¶

The estimable link between the hidden demand side and what SMARD publishes: grid-metered load equals a calendar/temperature demand model, minus PV self-consumption, plus noise.

Dgrid(t)=β0+βdow(t)+βholiday ⁣⋅ ⁣Holiday(t)+ftemp(T(t))+trend(t)⏟D^true(t;θ)  −  k⋅Ppv(t)+ε(t)D_{grid}(t) = \underbrace{\beta_0 + \beta_{dow}(t) + \beta_{holiday}\!\cdot\!Holiday(t) + f_{temp}(T(t)) + trend(t)}_{\hat D_{true}(t;\theta)} \; - \; k \cdot P_{pv}(t) + \varepsilon(t)Dgrid​(t)=D^true​(t;θ)β0​+βdow​(t)+βholiday​⋅Holiday(t)+ftemp​(T(t))+trend(t)​​−k⋅Ppv​(t)+ε(t)
(6)

The predictors actually used: day-of-week (dow(t)), hour-of-day (as a categorical, alongside dow), a German public-holiday indicator (Holiday(t)), heating- and cooling-degree terms built from area-average German temperature (T(t)), and a slow linear trend (trend(t)) for structural change over 2019-2025 (e.g. 2022’s demand destruction from the energy-price crisis).

Two honest gaps worth flagging rather than glossing over: temperature is area-average, not population-weighted — PECD does publish a population-weighted temperature product, but only at the pan-European synchronous-zone level, which can’t isolate Germany specifically, so area-average is what’s used instead. And there’s no explicit day-of-year / seasonal term beyond what temperature already captures — if there’s a seasonal demand pattern that isn’t just “hot” or “cold” (daylight-driven behavior, industrial calendars), the current model doesn’t have a dedicated term for it.

Why this regression is identifiable at all: P_pv(t) ≈ 0 at night and on heavily overcast days, so the -k·P_pv(t) term vanishes there — nighttime and overcast hours pin down θ (the calendar/temperature coefficients) with zero self-consumption interference. Daytime hours, where P_pv(t) varies a lot from cloud cover at otherwise-fixed calendar/weather conditions, then pin down k. One regression, not two sequential models.

The fitted self-consumption share comes out around 12% of national PV potential, net of the ~40% of the fleet registered full_feed_in (structurally excluded from self-consumption by regulation). Two built-in falsification checks were run and both came back non-zero rather than the expected zero — investigated rather than dismissed: the full_feed_in category’s own coefficient should be indistinguishable from zero but isn’t, because its regressor correlates at 0.95 with the one that should show an effect, leaving too little independent variation for OLS to separate them; and wind potential (no self-consumption channel at all) also came back significant, surviving a direct test of the obvious alternative (a much richer temperature response barely changed it) — more likely a real, different phenomenon: price-responsive industrial demand rising during cheap, high-wind hours. Full detail in the walkthrough.

Step 7: separating true oversupply from what curtailment already hid¶

To predict curtailment from an oversupply signal, that signal has to be built from things curtailment hasn’t already touched. The naive version isn’t:

OversupplyProxyobs(t)=Gwind_on(t)+Gwind_off(t)+Gpv(t)Dgrid(t)OversupplyProxy_{obs}(t) = \frac{G_{wind\_on}(t) + G_{wind\_off}(t) + G_{pv}(t)}{D_{grid}(t)}OversupplyProxyobs​(t)=Dgrid​(t)Gwind_on​(t)+Gwind_off​(t)+Gpv​(t)​
(7)

— built from already-curtailed observed generation, so it understates true oversupply exactly when curtailment is largest. The corrected version swaps in potential generation and estimated gross demand instead:

OversupplyProxypotential(t)=Ptotal(t)D^true(t)OversupplyProxy_{potential}(t) = \frac{P_{total}(t)}{\hat D_{true}(t)}OversupplyProxypotential​(t)=D^true​(t)Ptotal​(t)​
(8)

Step 8: the curtailment curve — calibrated on wind, transferred to PV¶

Gap_i(t) = P_i(t) - G_i(t) is directly computable from what’s already built. On wind, this gap is (almost) pure loss (Step 5) — a clean training signal for how curtailment responds to oversupply:

Gapwind,i(t)≈Lrepair,i(t)+Lprice,i(t)+Lcongestion,i(t)+εi(t),L^(t)=L^(OversupplyProxypotential(t))Gap_{wind,i}(t) \approx L_{repair,i}(t) + L_{price,i}(t) + L_{congestion,i}(t) + \varepsilon_i(t), \qquad \hat L(t) = \hat L\big(OversupplyProxy_{potential}(t)\big)Gapwind,i​(t)≈Lrepair,i​(t)+Lprice,i​(t)+Lcongestion,i​(t)+εi​(t),L^(t)=L^(OversupplyProxypotential​(t))
(9)

fit as an empirical bin-mean curtailment rate vs. oversupply, not an assumed functional form. Applying that same fitted curve to PV’s own oversupply hours, via PV’s own potential, predicts PV’s curtailment without needing to observe it directly — and whatever’s left of PV’s gap after subtracting it is an estimate of self-consumption plus PV’s own repair loss:

Gappv(t)−L^pv(t)  ≈  BTMpv(t)+Lrepair,pv(t)+εpv(t)Gap_{pv}(t) - \hat L_{pv}(t) \;\approx\; BTM_{pv}(t) + L_{repair,pv}(t) + \varepsilon_{pv}(t)Gappv​(t)−L^pv​(t)≈BTMpv​(t)+Lrepair,pv​(t)+εpv​(t)
(10)

The headline surprise: curtailment, not self-consumption, is the larger piece of PV’s own gap (76%) — not obvious going in, since self-consumption is the effect that usually gets talked about.

Two checks worth knowing about: onshore and offshore wind’s own curtailment-vs-oversupply curves (both BTM-free) agree from moderate oversupply upward — the range that matters for PV’s daylight hours, supporting the one-shared-curve transfer. And the leftover PV residual above correlates at 0.643 with an independent series the model never sees during fitting (MaStR’s self-consumption-registered capacity share) — supportive, though both simply trend upward together over 2019-2025, so treat it as suggestive rather than tightly validated.

Real redispatch data (SMARD, monthly, from July 2022) shows offshore wind curtailed roughly 8-9x more heavily per MW installed than onshore — a real technology-specific asymmetry the pooled onshore+offshore curve above currently discards (see notebook 44 for the full picture; that data validates an already-fitted curve after the fact, it can’t supply \hat L(t) a live forecasting input, since tomorrow’s curtailment is never observable at forecast time regardless of what data exists).

Step 9: the forecastable residual-load target¶

Realized residual load is a pure accounting identity — Step 2’s subtraction, nothing to estimate:

ResidualLoad(t)=Dgrid(t)−∑iGi(t)ResidualLoad(t) = D_{grid}(t) - \sum_i G_i(t)ResidualLoad(t)=Dgrid​(t)−i∑​Gi​(t)
(11)

The forecastable version substitutes gross demand and potential-minus-estimated-curtailment for the observed quantities, so it can be computed ahead of time from weather and calendar alone:

ResidualLoad^(t)=D^true(t)−(Ptotal(t)−L^(t))\widehat{ResidualLoad}(t) = \hat D_{true}(t) - \big(P_{total}(t) - \hat L(t)\big)ResidualLoad(t)=D^true​(t)−(Ptotal​(t)−L^(t))
(12)

Note BTM doesn’t appear here at all — it cancels out exactly as it does in the realized version, since the self-consumed MWh is absent from both published generation and published demand in equal measure. It still earned its place in Step 6: without the -k·P_pv(t) term, \hat D_{true}(t) itself would mistake the self-consumption dip for a real demand pattern.

Out-of-time — trained on 2019-2023, evaluated on 2024-2025:

ApproachMAEBiasCorr
Full model (this chapter)4,856 MW-1,936 MW0.915
Naive baseline (no self-consumption or curtailment correction)10,560 MW-9,750 MW0.905

Roughly half the MAE, a quarter of the bias — but a humbling result alongside it, worth stating plainly rather than only celebrating the win: a plain linear regression given the same inputs as the full structural model above, with none of its hand-built equations, does slightly better on this same backtest (MAE 4,177 MW, corr 0.941).

Most of the benefit in this backtest comes from having the right features at all (weather-derived potential, corrected for curtailment in some form) rather than from the specific structural extraction of D_true and the curtailment curve. That doesn’t make the structure wasted — it’s what makes the falsification checks, the wind/PV decomposition, and this chapter’s account of why the gap exists possible, none of which a flat regression offers — but as a pure point-forecast, it needed to earn its keep here and, on this specific test, didn’t quite. Step 10: an open tension — day-ahead price doesn’t see congestion, but \hat L(t) does¶

Germany’s day-ahead auction clears on a pre-redispatch basis — the price is set before grid operators intervene to relieve congestion. SMARD’s published generation, by contrast, is post-redispatch — it’s already had L_congestion applied. Every G_i(t) used throughout this chapter, and therefore ResidualLoad(t) and the \hat L(t) fit against it, is a post-redispatch quantity.

That’s fine for forecasting what actually reaches the grid. It’s a real problem if the eventual target shifts to forecasting the day-ahead price specifically: a price-relevant residual load should only net out L_repair and L_price — the losses that exist before the auction clears — not L_congestion, which happens after. But \hat L(t) in Step 8 is fit on wind’s total gap, because hourly per-loss-type data doesn’t exist (Step 8’s redispatch data is monthly and national-only). There’s currently no way to strip L_congestion back out of \hat L(t) to build that narrower, price-relevant target. Flagged here explicitly as unresolved, not swept into the roadmap below as if it were just more of the same work.

Step 11: mentioned, not modeled¶

  • Grid-scale storage (Step 1) — real, in the balance identity, not yet pulled into anything estimated here.

  • Behind-the-meter batteries paired with rooftop PV (Step 3) — turn BTM_{pv}(t) from a same-hour cancellation into a time-shifted one; not modeled.

  • Import/export beyond the aggregate NetImport(t) balance term — whether imported power is itself weather-driven (e.g. Nordic hydro or wind) matters for how “clean” a price-relevant residual load actually is, and isn’t yet understood in this project.

  • Hydro’s weather-driven share sitting inside G_other(t) rather than being split out like wind and PV (Step 2).

Roadmap: what a real forecast would chain together¶

If potential (P_i(t)), curtailment (\hat L(t)), and gross demand (\hat D_{true}(t)) can each be reconstructed well against historical weather, the same pipeline should run forward: feed it a weather forecast instead of realized weather, and \widehat{ResidualLoad}(t) for tomorrow falls out directly, without ever needing tomorrow’s curtailment or self-consumption to be observed. One caveat that belongs here, not in a footnote: every backtest number in Step 9 uses realized historical weather, not a forecast — it measures how well known weather translates into residual load, not genuine multi-day-ahead forecast skill. A real 5-15-day deployment would feed this same pipeline actual NWP forecast fields, and forecast error (especially wind-speed timing beyond a few days) stacks on top of everything measured here.

If the target moves from grid-injected residual load to day-ahead price, Step 10’s tension has to be resolved first — most likely by finding a way to separate L_congestion from L_price + L_repair rather than treating \hat L(t) as one bundled curve, or by explicitly quantifying how much error the current bundled target introduces when used as a price proxy.
