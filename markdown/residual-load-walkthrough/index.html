Walkthrough: estimating residual load

This page teaches the residual-load model built in notebooks 38-41 — what problem it solves, how to picture it, and what actually came out of fitting it. The full notation and the complete list of equations live on a separate reference page; this page tells the story, in the order it’s easiest to follow, and links to the reference and to the notebooks for the details it skips.

The problem, in one sentence¶

Residual load (demand minus renewable generation) drives prices, but both halves of that subtraction are things we never directly observe: gross power demand (grid-metered load, plus invisible behind-the-meter self-consumption) and renewable generation net of curtailment (what the weather could have produced, minus whatever grid operators or market conditions stopped from reaching the grid). We only ever see the already-contaminated versions of both.

Step 1: look at the gap directly¶

Before building anything, it’s worth just plotting potential generation (this project’s own PECD-methodology replication — see the wind and solar workstreams above) against what SMARD actually recorded reaching the grid.

Wind first, deliberately — not because it’s a clean match, but because it isolates one thing at a time:

This still is not apples-to-apples. The gap here is real and substantial — it opens up specifically during the high-output stretches where potential flattens out near the fleet’s ceiling, while observed generation stays well below it. Wind has no behind-the-meter self-consumption to blur the picture, so whatever gap remains is (almost) entirely curtailment plus a small amount of repair/model error — that’s what makes wind the clean calibration signal used below, not a reassuring “these should match” chart.

PV’s version of the same chart mixes in a second effect:

Every single day shows a gap at the peak, not just the high-oversupply days the way wind did — consistent with self-consumption being a constant companion to PV output, on top of the episodic curtailment effect wind already showed. Separating those two is the rest of this page.

Step 2: the mental model¶

Read this left to right: potential splits into three loss streams and whatever’s actually produced; produced splits again into what reaches the grid and what’s used behind the meter; and that behind-the-meter piece reappears on the demand side, combining with grid-metered demand into gross demand. Residual load only ever sees the grid boxes — which is exactly why BTM will turn out to cancel out of the final forecast target later on, even though it has to be modeled correctly along the way.

Putting real numbers on the PV side of that diagram:

This is the headline surprise of the whole exercise: curtailment, not self-consumption, is the larger piece of PV’s own gap (76%) — not obvious going in, since self-consumption is the effect that gets talked about, and curtailment the one that was only checked directly here.

Step 3: the equations that make this estimable¶

The full numbered list is on the reference page; the ones that matter for the story:

  • The physical balance (reference eq. 1-2): potential = losses + produced; produced = grid-injected + behind-the-meter.

  • The gross-demand regression (eq. 7): D_grid(t) as a calendar/temperature model minus k · P_pv(t) — identified because P_pv(t) = 0 at night and on heavily overcast days, pinning down the demand-model coefficients with zero self-consumption interference at all.

  • Two versions of the oversupply proxy (eq. 8 vs. 9) — this contrast is worth its own moment. The naive one (built from observed generation and load) understates true oversupply exactly when curtailment is largest, since curtailment has already suppressed its numerator. The corrected one swaps in potential generation and estimated gross demand instead — built from things curtailment hasn’t already touched.

  • The curtailment transfer (eq. 10): fit a curtailment-rate-vs- oversupply curve on wind’s clean gap, apply the same curve to PV.

  • The final target (eq. 11): BTM cancels out, as promised by the flow diagram above.

Step 4: evidence the method was actually tested, not just asserted¶

Three checks worth knowing about, because they’re what earns trust in the numbers above rather than just the numbers themselves:

  • Two built-in falsification checks, and both failed cleanly — informatively, not fatally. A PV feed-in category that structurally cannot self-consume should show a zero coefficient; it didn’t, and the reason turned out to be that its regressor correlates at 0.95 with the one that should show an effect, leaving too little independent variation for plain OLS to separate them. Wind potential, which has no self-consumption channel at all, also came back nonzero — but survived a direct test of the obvious alternative explanation (a much richer temperature response barely changed it), pointing instead at a real, different phenomenon: price-responsive industrial demand rising during cheap, high-wind hours. See notebook 38 for both in full.

  • Onshore and offshore wind were checked against each other (notebook 39) — both structurally BTM-free, so if their curtailment-vs-oversupply shapes diverged sharply, the “one shared curve” transfer to PV would be too coarse. They agree from moderate oversupply upward, which is the range that actually matters for PV’s own daylight hours.

  • The PV residual (curtailment already subtracted out) was checked against an independent series: the self-consumption-registered capacity share from MaStR, which the model never sees during fitting. They correlate at 0.643 — supportive, though both simply trend upward over 2019-2025, so treat this as suggestive rather than a tight validated match.

Step 6: does it actually work better?¶

Out-of-time — trained on 2019-2023, evaluated on 2024-2025, never seen during fitting:

ApproachMAEBiasCorr
Full model4,856 MW-1,936 MW0.915
Naive baseline10,560 MW-9,750 MW0.905

Roughly half the MAE, a quarter of the bias, only a modest correlation gain — consistent with the demand and curtailment corrections fixing the level far more than the day-to-day pattern, which both approaches already track reasonably well from shared weather inputs.

The caveat that belongs here, not in a footnote: this backtest uses realized historical weather, not a forecast. It measures how well the model translates known weather and calendar information into residual load — not genuine multi-day-ahead forecast skill. A real 5-15-day-ahead deployment would feed this same pipeline with actual NWP forecast fields, and forecast error (especially in wind speed timing beyond a few days) would stack on top of everything measured here. This project’s own physical translation (GWA bias correction, per-plant power curves, the technology-aware solar chain) is independently well-validated against real generation, which is a good reason to expect it improves the translation step of a forecast — but that’s a different, narrower claim than having tested actual multi-day forecast accuracy, which hasn’t been done.

One bug worth naming rather than quietly folding into the numbers above: building the comparison in Step 7 below exposed that the curtailment curve returned NaN for any out-of-time hour whose oversupply proxy fell outside the training period’s own range — 4.1% of the test period, silently dropped rather than handled. Fixed in pecdr/residual_load.py by clipping into the fitted range before lookup (the same graceful-degradation an out-of-range value gets from a tree-based model for free); every number on this page already reflects the fix.

Step 7: does the structure actually earn its keep?¶

Step 6 answers “is the full model better than doing nothing.” It doesn’t answer a sharper question: does explicitly modeling the unobserved quantities (D_true, the curtailment curve) beat simply handing the same observed weather-derived features to a model and letting it find its own answer? Notebook 42 adds four more points to the same out-of-time backtest to separate that out:

ModelWhat it usesMAECorr
M0: calendar onlyNo weather at all10,284 MW0.486
M1: naive baselineTemperature + raw potential, no curtailment correction10,560 MW0.905
M2: full structuralD_true, curtailment curve — the model taught above4,856 MW0.915
M3: linear, features onlySame features as M2, one flat regression, no structure4,177 MW0.941
M4: LightGBM, features onlySame features as M3, gradient-boosted trees6,714 MW0.921
M5: LightGBM, capacity-rescaledSame as M4, but capacity factors as features and a capacity-normalized target5,294 MW0.893

Four findings here are worth sitting with, because none of them is the “obviously expected” result:

  • M0 vs. M1: weather information, used carelessly, is not obviously better than no weather information at all. M1 has much better correlation than M0 (0.905 vs. 0.486 — it clearly tracks the pattern better), but its uncorrected curtailment assumption produces a bias so large that its absolute error ends up slightly worse than the calendar-only floor’s.

  • M2 vs. M3: a plain linear regression, given the same inputs as the full structural model but none of its equations, does better. Lower MAE, higher correlation. The most likely reason: M2’s curtailment curve is fit on wind and transferred to PV under a shared-curve assumption, while M3’s regression fits PV’s own relationship to its own history directly, with no transfer step to be wrong about.

  • M3 vs. M4: more flexibility didn’t help, for a specific, checked reason, not a mysterious one. Installed capacity grew throughout 2019-2025, so years_since_start has 100% of test-period rows beyond the training range and P_wind_off_mw has 20% — tree-based models cannot extrapolate a trend past what they saw in training, while a linear trend term does so by construction.

  • M4 vs. M5: rescaling by installed capacity helps, but doesn’t fully fix it, and checking why turned up a second mechanism, not a dead end. M5 (capacity factors as features, a capacity-normalized target, rescaled back to MW by multiplying by the actual, looked-up capacity at test time) cuts M4’s MAE from 6,714 to 5,294 MW — the extrapolation story was real. But it still trails M3, because a different problem takes its place: M5’s error in the normalized domain is tiny and essentially zero in-sample, but picks up a small (~0.006) out-of-sample bias on the test period — and multiplying that small bias back by TotalCapacity_mw to reconstruct MW amplifies it, precisely because capacity is largest late in the test period. Checked directly: the signed MW error correlates with TotalCapacity_mw at 0.44. Rescaling trades the tree’s input-side extrapolation problem for a smaller output-side amplification problem — a real improvement, not a full fix.

So which is it — features, or structure? In this backtest, mostly features: the biggest jump is M1→M2/M3 (weather-derived potential, corrected for curtailment in some form), while M2→M3 shows the specific hand-built structural form isn’t what’s earning that improvement, and a simpler model given the same information does slightly better. That doesn’t make the structural work wasted — it’s what made the falsification checks, the wind/PV decomposition, and the equations page’s account of why the gap exists possible, none of which a flat regression can offer — but as a pure point-forecast for residual load, the more complex approach needed to earn its keep here, and on this specific test, it didn’t quite.

What’s still open¶

  • The price-responsive-demand confound flagged in notebook 38.

  • The un-detrended self-consumption cross-check in notebook 39 (year-over-year differences instead of levels would be a stronger test than the raw correlation).

  • M5’s own remaining gap: its out-of-sample bias in the normalized domain (~0.006) is small but not zero, and gets amplified on reconstruction to MW — worth understanding further (a rolling/expanding retraining scheme, discussed a few turns back, would keep that bias smaller by keeping the tree closer to the range it’s asked to predict).

  • Validating the curtailment estimate against real Redispatch 2.0 data (available from October 2021) — deliberately deferred; see the reference page for why real congestion data can only validate an already-estimated model, never serve as a live forecasting input.

  • Testing this pipeline against real NWP forecast data at 5-15-day lead times, to turn the “known-weather” backtest above into an actual forecast-skill measurement.
