How much curtailment actually happens, and when?

Before building any model of curtailment, it’s worth just looking at real ground truth: since July 2022, SMARD publishes how much feed-in was reduced or increased under Germany’s Redispatch 2.0 regime, broken down by energy carrier. This notebook is purely descriptive -- no modeling, no comparison to this project’s own estimates (see pecdr/smard_redispatch.py and book/markdown/congestion_curtailment_data.md for how the data is fetched and what it can/can’t tell us).

Two important scope limits to keep in view throughout: this is monthly, national-only data (no hour-of-day or regional detail), and it’s redispatch specifically -- grid-operator-ordered curtailment, not market-based (negative-price) curtailment, which is a separate mechanism this data doesn’t capture at all.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import pandas as pd

from pecdr.paths import ProjPaths

paths = ProjPaths()
paths.ensure_directories()

redispatch = pd.read_parquet(paths.redispatch_by_source_file)
RENEWABLE_SOURCES = ["Wind_Onshore", "Wind_Offshore", "Photovoltaik"]
colors = plt.rcParams["axes.prop_cycle"].by_key()["color"]

Reductions vs. increases: a quick sanity check on semantics¶

“Reduction” is curtailment-down (feed-in cut below what a plant could have produced); “increase” is counter-trading up-regulation (a plant asked to produce more to compensate elsewhere on the grid). If the data means what it says, increases should be dominated by conventional/gas plants, since curtailing renewables and then also asking them to ramp up doesn’t make physical sense -- renewables should appear almost exclusively on the reduction side.

totals_by_direction = redispatch.groupby(["direction", "energy_source"])["gwh"].sum().unstack("direction").fillna(0.0)
totals_by_direction["reduction_share_of_total"] = totals_by_direction["reduction"] / (
    totals_by_direction["reduction"] + totals_by_direction["increase"]
)
totals_by_direction.sort_values("reduction", ascending=False)

Worth noting directly: wind offshore’s cumulative curtailment (16,108 GWh) is actually larger than wind onshore’s (12,873 GWh), despite offshore’s installed fleet being only about a seventh the size of onshore’s (~9.2 GW vs. ~63.1 GW in 2025 -- see pecdr.paths.ProjPaths.residual_load_inputs_file’s Capacity_wind_off_mw/Capacity_wind_on_mw columns). Per MW installed, that’s roughly 8-9x more curtailment for offshore than onshore over this period -- consistent with known transmission bottlenecks moving northern offshore generation south to load centers.

When during the year does curtailment happen?¶

The closest this monthly data can get to “when”: averaging by calendar month across the ~3.5 years covered, separately per renewable technology.

reduction_renewables = reduction.loc[reduction["energy_source"].isin(RENEWABLE_SOURCES)].copy()
reduction_renewables["calendar_month"] = reduction_renewables["month"].dt.month
seasonal = reduction_renewables.groupby(["energy_source", "calendar_month"])["gwh"].mean().unstack("energy_source")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 5))

Takeaways¶

  • Wind dominates curtailment volume, and offshore is curtailed far more heavily relative to its fleet size than onshore -- a real, measured asymmetry this project’s model doesn’t currently distinguish (the curtailment curve in notebooks 39/40/42 is calibrated on pooled onshore+offshore wind, then transferred to PV as well).

  • PV curtailment is real but smaller in absolute terms, and clearly seasonal, peaking alongside PV’s own generation season rather than at some independent time.

  • This is a lower bound on total curtailment, not the whole picture: it only captures grid-operator-ordered redispatch, not market-based (negative-price) curtailment, which this data source doesn’t report at all.

  • The natural next question -- does this real data actually support the wind-to-PV curve-transfer assumption used in the residual-load model, or does it call for a PV-specific curve -- is deliberately left open here; this chapter’s only job was building a feel for the data itself.
