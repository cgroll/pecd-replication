Offshore wind: does curtailment explain the potential-observed gap?

The previous chapter showed that netztransparenz’s per-measure export and SMARD’s monthly series agree almost exactly (100%) on offshore-wind curtailment volume -- unusually clean confirmation for a technology whose entities are named individually in the per-measure file. With both curtailment sources trusted, this chapter asks the next question: the residual-load walkthrough shows offshore wind’s raw potential-minus-observed gap and calls it “(almost) entirely loss” -- there’s no behind-the-meter term to blur it, unlike PV. Does real (redispatch) curtailment actually explain that raw gap? And for whatever’s left over, do negative day-ahead prices -- which can drive plant operators to curtail voluntarily, entirely outside of any grid-congestion redispatch instruction -- explain the rest?

Which relationship is actually tighter?¶

Eyeballing two scatter plots isn’t a comparison -- computing the same metrics against both puts a number on the visual difference above.

def scatter_metrics(potential: pd.Series, actual: pd.Series) -> dict:
    corr = np.corrcoef(potential, actual)[0, 1]
    slope, intercept = np.polyfit(potential, actual, 1)
    residual = actual - potential
    ss_res_identity = (residual ** 2).sum()
    ss_tot = ((actual - actual.mean()) ** 2).sum()
    return {
        "correlation_r": round(corr, 4),
        "regression_slope": round(slope, 4),
        "r2_vs_y_equals_x": round(1 - ss_res_identity / ss_tot, 4),
        "mae_mwh_per_day": round(residual.abs().mean(), 0),
        "rmse_mwh_per_day": round(np.sqrt((residual ** 2).mean()), 0),
        "sum_ratio_actual_over_potential": round(actual.sum() / potential.sum(), 4),
    }

metrics_comparison = pd.DataFrame({
    "observed_only": scatter_metrics(potential_daily, observed_daily),
    "observed_plus_curtailment": scatter_metrics(potential_daily, reconstructed_daily),
})

Adding real curtailment back onto observed makes the relationship to potential meaningfully tighter on every one of these metrics, not just one favorable one: correlation rises from 0.94 to 0.97, the R² against y = x itself (not a best-fit line -- literally “how close to matching potential exactly”) nearly quadruples from 0.21 to 0.85, and the typical daily error (RMSE) drops by almost half, from ~35,200 to ~19,900 MWh/day. So real curtailment is doing real, substantial work in explaining the potential-observed gap -- it closes a large part of it (the R² jump is the clearest evidence of that) -- it just doesn’t close all of it. The more likely explanation for the rest is bias in this project’s own “potential” replication (notebooks 31-36’s offshore bias-correction chain): P_wind_off_mw is itself an estimate built from bias-corrected wind speed and a power curve, not a direct measurement, so a systematic overestimate there would show up here as apparent “gap” that no amount of real curtailment data could ever close. The “(almost) entirely loss” framing in the residual-load walkthrough is too strong for offshore wind -- a substantial share of that gap is more likely replication bias than real curtailment. Before accepting that conclusion, though, there’s one more real-world source of loss this chapter hasn’t touched yet: curtailment a plant chooses voluntarily, without any redispatch instruction at all, when the day-ahead price goes negative.

Do negative prices explain the rest?¶

Redispatch is a TSO-instructed measure, issued for grid-congestion reasons. But a wind-offshore operator facing a negative day-ahead price has its own incentive to produce less regardless of congestion -- selling into a negative price means paying to feed in. That kind of price-driven throttling would never show up in either curtailment source used above (both are congestion/redispatch specific), yet it would still open up a gap between potential and observed.

Quantifying the impact¶

The split above shows that negative prices matter; it doesn’t say how much of a day’s gap they typically account for. Rather than bolting on another hand-built formula, the natural way to quantify a relationship like this is a plain regression: pick an outcome and an explanatory variable, and see how strongly, and by how much, one moves with the other.

  • Outcome -- residual_after_curtailment: potential - observed - actual curtailment, clipped at 0. Whatever’s left of the raw gap once real redispatch curtailment is subtracted out. This is the same quantity the curtailment-only section above already plots; it just hasn’t been isolated as its own series until now.

  • Explanatory variable -- negative-price exposure: that day’s potential (MWh) summed over just the hours when the price was negative. This measures opportunity, not what actually happened -- how much production a fully price-responsive operator could have chosen to forgo that day, independent of what was separately curtailed for congestion reasons.

If price-driven curtailment is a real, sizeable effect, days with more exposure should have a larger unexplained residual, and a line fit through the scatter should have a positive, non-trivial slope: each extra MWh of potential exposed to a negative price should show up, at least partially, as extra unexplained gap. The slope itself has a direct reading -- it’s the fraction of “at-risk” potential that typically ends up unrealized, on top of whatever curtailment already covers.

One honest caveat before looking at the numbers: negative prices and high wind output tend to occur together (oversupply is what drives prices negative in the first place), and this project’s own offshore potential replication was already flagged, in the curtailment-only section above, as least trustworthy on exactly those high-output days. So a positive slope here is consistent with a real price effect, but it can’t fully rule out that some of it is the same replication-bias effect resurfacing, correlated with the same high-wind days that also happen to have negative prices.

residual_after_curtailment_daily = (implied_loss_daily - offshore_curtailment_daily).clip(lower=0)
negative_price_exposure_daily = inputs["P_wind_off_mw"].where(price < 0, 0.0).resample("D").sum().reindex(daily_range)

Does a joint model beat curtailment alone?¶

The two pieces above were checked one at a time. The direct test of whether negative-price exposure earns its place alongside curtailment, rather than as a separate add-on, is a single regression with both as predictors -- comparing that regression’s fit to the curtailment-only version isolates exactly what exposure contributes once curtailment is already in the model.

regression_daily = pd.DataFrame({
    "gap": implied_loss_daily,
    "curtailment": offshore_curtailment_daily,
    "exposure": negative_price_exposure_daily,
}).reindex(daily_range)

model_curtailment_only = smf.ols("gap ~ curtailment", data=regression_daily).fit()
model_curtailment_and_price = smf.ols("gap ~ curtailment + exposure", data=regression_daily).fit()

print(f"gap ~ curtailment                : R² = {model_curtailment_only.rsquared:.3f}")
print(f"gap ~ curtailment + exposure      : R² = {model_curtailment_and_price.rsquared:.3f}")

Adding exposure to the regression raises the gap model’s own R² from 0.68 to 0.76 -- a real jump, not a rounding difference, from a single extra variable. Its coefficient (~0.44) is close to the ~0.43 slope found fitting exposure on its own further up, and it’s estimated much more precisely than chance (p < 0.001) even with curtailment already in the model -- exposure isn’t just standing in for curtailment.

From the regression back to potential: three models, side by side¶

Turning this into a reconstructed series takes one more decision: the regression above has an intercept (~12,900 MWh/day), representing the average gap left over even at zero curtailment and zero exposure. That intercept isn’t attributable to price -- by definition it’s whatever’s left when exposure is zero -- so folding it into "observed + curtailment

  • price" would credit negative prices with a lift that has nothing to do with negative prices, and would add that same lift to every day, exposed or not. To keep the price contribution honestly isolated, only the exposure slope is added back on top of observed + curtailment, and it’s re-fit through the origin (no intercept) so that a day with zero exposure gets exactly zero adjustment:

reconstructed = observed + curtailment + slope * exposure   (slope fit with no intercept)

Comparing all three reconstructions against potential with the same metrics used throughout this chapter shows the same progression from a different angle -- the closer this table’s numbers get to a perfect match (r = 1, R² = 1, MAE/RMSE = 0, sum ratio = 1), the better.

On individual high-exposure days the reconstruction can still land above potential (nothing forces it not to), but it no longer does so systematically across the whole sample the way an intercept-based version would.

Takeaways¶

  • Negative prices do carry real signal. Splitting the curtailment-only model’s own fit by whether a day had a negative-price hour shows a materially worse fit on negative-price days (R² against y = x: 0.89 vs. 0.60; RMSE roughly doubles) -- a model-free result, not dependent on any equation for how price-driven curtailment should work.

  • The regression puts a number on the effect. Regressing the curtailment-unexplained residual on negative-price exposure gives a slope of ~0.43 (r = 0.51, R² = 0.26): each extra MWh of potential exposed to a negative price is associated with roughly 0.43 MWh of additional unexplained gap. The fit line’s intercept (~12,600 MWh/day) is also informative -- it’s the average unexplained residual even on days with zero negative-price exposure, i.e. the part of the gap this variable can’t touch at all. Both the moderate R² and the sizeable intercept say the same thing as the split test above from a different angle: negative prices are a real, non-trivial piece of the story, not the dominant one.

  • Exposure earns its place alongside curtailment, not just on its own. Putting both into one regression raises the gap model’s R² from 0.68 to 0.76, with exposure’s coefficient precisely estimated even after curtailment is already in the model. Turned back into a reconstruction -- adding only the exposure slope, fit through the origin, so days with no exposure get no adjustment -- the three progressively richer models (observed alone, + curtailment, + curtailment and price exposure) improve on every metric in turn (R² against y = x: 0.21 -> 0.85 -> 0.90; RMSE: ~35,200 -> ~19,900 -> ~16,700 MWh/day; sum_ratio: 0.74 -> 0.87 -> 0.90). Unlike a naive intercept-based version, this reconstruction only moves on days that actually had negative-price exposure -- visible directly in the Dec 2023 zoom above.

  • This isn’t the final word. The exposure variable is deliberately simple (potential during negative-price hours, not accounting for how negative, or interacting with curtailment) and the confounding caveat above still applies -- some of this relationship could be the offshore potential replication’s own high-wind bias showing up a second time, not a pure price effect. Both curtailment and negative prices are real, measurable contributors to the gap; what’s left over after both is more likely a mix of that replication bias and this simplified model’s own blind spots than a further unmeasured real-world loss mechanism.
